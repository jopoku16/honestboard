\begin{abstract}
Leaderboards for AI agents announce a record whenever a submission scores higher than every earlier one on a fixed set of tasks.
A benchmark is only a sample of tasks, so some records are luck.
We certify records as they arrive.
The per-step test is exact on the tasks where the new system and the old record disagree, allows for the step being tested only because it is a record, and, as we prove, stays valid although the old record was itself the best so far.
Three rules for the level at each step provably keep the chance of ever certifying a false record at most 5\%, whatever the number of records and the dependence between them.
On the \NumTotalSteps{} records of six public agent leaderboards, \NumTotalSig{} (\NumTotalSigPct\%) are clearly better than the record they replaced, \NumTotalHolm{} after a correction for many tests, and the rules certify \NumCertTotalSpend{}; a split-half check on the real data is consistent with the test separating genuine records from lucky ones.
The reason is resolution: SWE-bench Verified can reliably see gaps of about \NumVerifiedMDD{} points, its typical record wins by \NumVerifiedMedianMargin{}, one point would need about \NumVerifiedTasksOnePt{} tasks, and \NumVerifiedSet{} of its \NumVerifiedK{} systems could be the true best.
The top score is inflated by being picked as the highest (\NumVBunchNCNaiveBias{} points with equal leaders); a bootstrap correction removes about \NumVBunchNCReduction\% of it.
Tuning on public tasks is not modelled.
We release \texttt{honestboard}, a Python package that computes all of this from task-by-task results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A leaderboard takes a big table of results, one row per system and one column per task, and boils it down to one score per system and one ranking.
Everyone then uses that ranking: papers claim a new state of the art, companies compare products, and users pick tools.
The ranking is treated as a fact, but it is really an estimate.
SWE-bench Verified~\citep{jimenez2024swebench} has 500 tasks.
Two agents that differ by a point and a half on those tasks actually disagree on only a few dozen of them, and which agent wins on those few depends on which tasks happened to be in the benchmark.

We call a \emph{record step} a submission that scores higher than every earlier submission on its leaderboard, in date order; the \emph{record it replaced} is the previous record holder.
Using the task-by-task results that several leaderboards publish, we ask four questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Records.} When a submission sets a new record, is it really better than the record it replaced, or could the gap be luck?
\item \textbf{Resolution.} What is the smallest true gap a benchmark can reliably see, and how does that compare with the gaps records are claimed by?
\item \textbf{Ranks.} How much does the top of the ranking move if we redraw the tasks, and which systems might really be the best?
\item \textbf{Winner's curse.} The top score was picked because it was the highest of many noisy scores. How much does it overstate how good the system really is, and can we correct for that?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Findings.}
Across \NumTotalSteps{} record steps on \NumBoardsWithSteps{} leaderboards, \NumTotalSig{} (\NumTotalSigPct\%) are clearly better than the record they replaced, using a test that allows for the fact that we only look at records because they scored higher; \NumTotalHolm{} (\NumTotalHolmPct\%) remain after a correction for testing many records on one board; and \NumClusterSig{} of the \NumClusterSteps{} SWE-bench records remain when code repositories, not tasks, are treated as the unit that was sampled (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
The pattern follows the size of the benchmark: SWE-bench Test, with \NumTestT{} tasks, confirms \NumTestSig{} of its \NumTestSteps{} records, while the \NumSmallBoards{} boards with at most 510 tasks confirm \NumSmallSig{} of \NumSmallSteps{}.
Figure~\ref{fig:forest} shows every record on four boards and Figure~\ref{fig:resolution} shows why: on these boards a typical record wins by less than the smallest gap the board can reliably see.
Among 135 submissions, pure luck would produce about \NumVerifiedNoiseRecords{} records; Verified has \NumVerifiedSteps{}, so the leaderboard does track real progress, but most single records are too small for the board to confirm.
Of the \NumClaimsReport{} Verified records whose leaderboard entry links a write-up (a paper, technical report or blog post, as declared by the submitter), only \NumClaimsReportSig{} can be told apart from the record it replaced (Table~\ref{tab:verifiedchain}).
Rankings wobble in the same way: two systems tie for first on Verified, the one that ranks first most often does so in only \NumVerifiedPBest{} of redraws of the tasks, and \NumVerifiedSet{} of the \NumVerifiedK{} systems could be the true best.
Terminal-Bench~2 has a clear leader (first in \NumTBPBest{} of redraws), so the tools do not always say ``noise''.
They work the same way on a leaderboard of plain language models: on LiveBench (\NumLBT{} questions) \NumLBSig{} of \NumLBSteps{} records pass the basic test and \NumLBHolm{} passes the correction for many tests, too few records to compare with the agent boards (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
The winner's reported score is inflated, and a bootstrap correction removes most of the inflation when we check on tasks held back at random, with the caveats in Section~\ref{sec:valid}.
None of this means the records are fake or that two systems are equal: a record that we cannot confirm may still be better; the data just cannot show it.

\paragraph{Relation to earlier work.}
Careful statistics for AI evaluation is not new: error bars for language-model scores~\citep{miller2024errorbars,madaan2024variance,bowyer2025clt,heineman2025signal}, power calculations for benchmark comparisons~\citep{card2020power}, comparisons of agents that account for cost~\citep{kapoor2024agents}, run-to-run intervals for coding agents~\citep{huang2026deepswe}, the effect of the scaffolding around a model~\citep{zhang2026harness,vats2026scaffold,arjmandi2026harness}, and tests for whether a new model version got worse~\citep{kubler2026degradation}.
Table~\ref{tab:related} sets this paper beside the five lines of work closest to it; the level rules for records that arrive one by one are the alpha-spending and online fallback rules of the sequential-testing literature, which take valid $p$-values as given, and what is new here is a $p$-value for a record step that stays valid under the selection the chain itself performs, which is what those rules need as input.
Rank intervals for leaderboards are developed by \citet{neuhof2026rankintervals,neuhof2026ranking}, with roots in \citet{mogstad2024ranks} and \citet{hung2019rank}; sets of possible best models in \citet{hansen2011mcs} and \citet{kim2025confidence}; Chatbot Arena reports bootstrap intervals and rank sets~\citep{chiang2024arena,chatzi2024ppr}.
On the winner's curse, \citet{andrews2024winners}, \citet{zrnic2025flexible}, \citet{petrouzeniou2024ranking} and \citet{hoff2025selective} give corrections for a winner that has already been picked, and \citet{bakshi2026winners} one that adds randomness; we use the conditional method of \citet{lee2016exact,fithian2014optimal} and an iterated bootstrap~\citep{efron2011tweedie,efron2014model}, and run the hybrid of \citet{andrews2024winners} and the zoom interval of \citet{zrnic2025flexible} as baselines.
Inflation from teams privately trying many versions before submitting~\citep{singh2025illusion,yang2026hidden} and from overfitting to a benchmark~\citep{roelofs2019meta} are the kinds of error this paper does not model; an informal bootstrap analysis of Terminal-Bench~2 that grouped tasks reached a similar conclusion about its top two entries~\citep{lesswrong2026tbench}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper has one piece of new statistics, an audit built on it, and a tool.
(i) The new statistics: a way to certify records as they arrive. We give a $p$-value for a record step that stays valid even though we only test a step because it is a record and even though the old record was itself the best of everything before it (Proposition~1); two rules for setting the level at each step, alpha-spending and the online fallback rule of \citet{tian2021onlinefwer}, that keep the chance of ever certifying a false record at most 5\% no matter how records depend on each other (Corollaries~1 and 2; the rules and their guarantees are standard, and what is proved here is that the record $p$-value meets the condition they need); the same guarantee for the version in which the leader only changes when a record is certified (Corollary~3); and an online false-discovery-rate variant checked by simulation (Section~\ref{sec:methods}). As far as we know this is the first certification rule for leaderboard records with a guarantee of this kind, and Table~\ref{tab:verifiedchain} shows it running over the full history of SWE-bench Verified.
(ii) The audit: every record on six public agent leaderboards, checked with that test and with a repository-level test, compared against what each benchmark can resolve; a split-half check across tasks and a run-split check across repeated runs on the real data; a language-model leaderboard run through the same pipeline; and a bound on how much reporting the best of several runs would inflate a score, from the one board that has repeated runs.
(iii) Rank intervals and a set of possible best systems, with a check of how often they contain the truth, and a corrected score for the published winner, checked by simulation and on held-back tasks with error bars for the simulation itself, against the hybrid of \citet{andrews2024winners} and the zoom interval of \citet{zrnic2025flexible}.
What is proved and what is only simulated: the per-step test holds its level under its stated null (exactly, at the attainable levels, for a fixed holder), and the error over a whole chain is controlled for three rules; everything else, including LORD++, the winner correction and the set of possible best systems, rests on simulation and held-back data. Table~\ref{tab:guarantees} in Section~\ref{sec:methods} says which is which.
(iv) \texttt{honestboard}, a small Python package with a command line and a check for new submissions,\footnote{Version 0.1.0, MIT licence: \url{https://github.com/jopoku16/honestboard}, with the built boards, every result file, the scripts behind each table and figure, and the manuscript source.} and a short list of things leaderboards should report.

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}
A benchmark has $T$ tasks and $K$ systems.
System $k$ gets a result $y_{kt}\in[0,1]$ on task $t$: a pass (1) or fail (0) when each task is run once, or the share of repeated runs that passed.
The reported score is the average, $\hat p_k=T^{-1}\sum_t y_{kt}$.
What we actually want to know is $\theta_k=\E[y_{kt}]$: the score system $k$, exactly as submitted, would get on average over all tasks of the kind the benchmark samples from.
In other words, we treat the tasks as the thing that was sampled.
Every system is scored on the same tasks, so the scores move together (a hard task is hard for everyone), and every comparison between two systems is a paired comparison, task by task.
We estimate how the scores co-vary by $\hat\Sigma_{ij}=\{T(T-1)\}^{-1}\sum_t (y_{it}-\bar y_i)(y_{jt}-\bar y_j)$.
Where tasks come in groups (the code repository they belong to) we also use a version of this that sums within groups, and a bootstrap that resamples groups.

\paragraph{What the numbers are about.}
Everything below is about $\theta_k$ in that sense: the score a system would get on more tasks of the same kind as the benchmark's (the same repositories, the same kinds of problems, the same scoring).
It is not about repositories the benchmark does not contain, about real-world use, or about a system after it has been tuned further.
A reported score has three parts: the true score $\theta_k$, luck in which tasks were drawn, and inflation from development, because teams tune their systems, and sometimes train them, with the public tasks in view~\citep{singh2025illusion,yang2026hidden}.
With one run per system we can only measure the second part.
Table~\ref{tab:scope} says which part each of our procedures handles.
Inflation from development and the randomness of an agent from one run to the next are not modelled; both push reported scores up, so a winner may be more inflated than our correction removes.
Section~\ref{sec:audit} puts a number on one form of development inflation, reporting the best of several runs, using the Terminal-Bench submissions that ran the whole benchmark more than once.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\paragraph{Record test.}
Let $a$ be the record holder and $b$ a new submission with a higher score, $\hat p_b>\hat p_a$.
Tasks that both solved or both failed tell us nothing about which is better, so we look only at the tasks where they disagree: $n_b$ tasks solved only by $b$ and $n_a$ solved only by $a$, with $n=n_a+n_b$ in total.
If the two systems were equally good, each of these $n$ tasks would be a coin flip, so the natural test asks whether $n_b$ wins out of $n$ is more than a fair coin would give.
This is the binomial (sign) test, the exact form of McNemar's test~\citep{mcnemar1947}.
There is a catch: we only look at this step \emph{because} $b$ won more of the disputed tasks ($n_b>n_a$), and that alone makes the count look impressive.
So we run the test given that fact: with $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\tfrac12)$ the number of heads in $n$ fair coin flips, the \emph{selective} $p$-value is
\[p_{\mathrm{sel}}=P(X\ge n_b\mid X>n/2)=\frac{P(X\ge n_b)}{P(X>n/2)}.\]
In words: among all the ways a fair coin could have given $b$ the majority, how often would it have given $b$ at least this many wins?
A $p$-value is the chance of seeing a result at least this extreme if the two systems were really equal; a small one (we use $0.05$, one in twenty) means luck is an unlikely explanation.
If the holder were fixed in advance and the null held (on each disputed task $b$ is no likelier than $a$ to be the one that passes), $p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ would be a valid $p$-value exactly; Proposition~1 below shows it stays valid when the holder is itself the best of everything before it.
For odd $n$ it equals the usual two-sided exact $p$-value.
For even $n$ it is larger, because $P(X>n/2)=\{1-P(X=n/2)\}/2<\tfrac12$: the two-sided test then rejects too often among record steps (with $n=44$ its conditional size is 5.5\%), and a one-sided test at level $\alpha$ rejects about $2\alpha$ of them.
We call a step significant when $p_{\mathrm{sel}}\le0.05$; the two-sided and one-sided tests at 5\% are reported as sensitivity checks.
Section~\ref{sec:valid} checks by simulation that the test rejects no more than 5\% of true-null steps, under two nulls and by the step's position in the chain, and repeats the test on split halves of the real boards.
When a board has many record steps, some will pass at 5\% by chance; Holm's correction~\citep{holm1979} raises the bar so that the chance of \emph{any} false pass on one board stays at 5\%.
For Terminal-Bench, where each $y_{kt}$ is a rate rather than a pass or fail, we use the sign-flip version: randomly flip the sign of each task's difference many times ($2\times10^5$ sign patterns, the same patterns in the audit and in the certification, so that a $p$-value near 0.001 has a Monte Carlo standard error of about $10^{-4}$) and compute $P(S\ge s_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid S>0)$ for the sum $S$; this one is approximate.
Tasks from the same code repository are not independent (an agent that handles one repository well tends to handle its other tasks well too), so the task-level test is the optimistic one and the repository-level test below the cautious one; Section~\ref{sec:valid} measures the difference.

\emph{Submissions sharing a date.}
Under the best-first ordering (described below) the challenger at a record step is the best of all submissions that share its date.
That is a second kind of selection the record event does not cover: in a simulation where no system is better than any other and 20 submissions share a date, the per-step test rejects about 10\% of record steps instead of 5\%.
We therefore multiply the $p$-value of a record step by $g$, the number of submissions on the challenger's date, the standard Bonferroni fix for picking the best of $g$ (the package's tests include that simulation); a submission with a date of its own has $g=1$.
On the real boards \NumTotalDateGroupSteps{} of the \NumTotalSteps{} record steps have $g$ between 2 and 4; without the correction \NumTotalSigUncorrected{} steps are significant, with it \NumTotalSig{}.

\paragraph{Repository-level record test.}
For boards that label each task with its code repository we also run a repository-level test: add up the per-task differences within each repository, then randomly flip the sign of each repository total (all $2^G$ patterns when there are $G\le 14$ repositories).
Its selective form conditions on a positive sum in the same way.
It is valid as long as the repository totals are equally likely to be positive or negative under the null; with only a dozen repositories it has little power, which is why the task-level count stays the headline and the repository-level count sits beside it as the cautious one.

\paragraph{Ordering submissions that share a date.}
Which steps count as records depends on how we order submissions with the same date.
The main analysis orders them best first: this gives the fewest and biggest steps, never invents a record that was not on the leaderboard, and is the most generous reading for the board.
We also report worst first, which gives the most steps but can create records that never actually appeared, so we treat it only as a bound.
The best-first challenger is the best of its date, which is the selection the per-date factor above corrects for.

\paragraph{Certifying records as they arrive.}
A leaderboard has to decide about each record when it comes in, without knowing how many records will follow, so Holm over the finished chain is only something you can do looking back.
We give a procedure for the live setting.
For the $i$-th record step, with holder $a$ and challenger $b$, let $p_i=p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ be the selective $p$-value of the record test.
With a fixed holder it is a valid $p$-value given that the step is a record, because the record event is exactly $\{X>n/2\}$.
A holder that is the best so far got there partly by luck, so its results are tilted upward; this works in our favour, making $p_i$ if anything too cautious, and it is a theorem, not only a simulation.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Proposition 1.}
Let the outcome vectors of the $K$ systems be independent across tasks, and let the \emph{conditional task-wise dominance null} hold: for each task, given the task and the outcomes of every system other than $a$ and $b$ (earlier and later), the probability that $b$ passes and $a$ fails is at most the probability that $a$ passes and $b$ fails.
(Equality is the null of the exact test, no task-level advantage for $b$; the null implies $\theta_b\le\theta_a$ but is not implied by it.)
Let $A$ be any event that does not involve the outcomes of $b$ and that, given the outcomes of the other systems, is increasing in the outcomes of $a$ task by task; the event that $a$ holds the record among the earlier submissions, which depends on $a$ only through its total, is one.
Then, conditional on $A$, on the record event $\{n_b>n_a\}$, on the discordant set and on the outcomes outside it, $P(p_i\le u)\le u$ for every $u\in[0,1]$.
\end{quote}
In plain terms: under the stated null and with tasks independent, knowing that the holder is ``the best so far'' (or anything else that only gets more likely as the holder does better) can only make the $p$-value more cautious, never less.
The proof has three steps: under the null the challenger's win count on the disputed tasks behaves like at most a fair coin; knowing the holder did well pushes that count further down; and a count pushed down gives a larger $p$-value.

\emph{Proof.}
Fix the discordant set $D$ with $|D|=n$, the outcomes of $a$ and $b$ outside $D$ and the outcomes of every other system.
By independence across tasks, given these the $n$ tasks of $D$ are independently $b$-only with probabilities at most one half, so the law of $n_b$ is dominated in the likelihood-ratio order by $\mathrm{Bin}(n,\tfrac12)$: Bernoulli laws are log-concave, and the likelihood-ratio order is preserved under convolution of log-concave laws~\citep[Section~1.C]{shaked2007stochastic}.
Within $D$, $a$ passes exactly the tasks that are not $b$-only, so $A$ is a decreasing event in the vector of $b$-only indicators, and by Efron's monotonicity theorem for independent variables with log-concave laws~\citep{efron1965polya}, $P(A\mid n_b=k,\dots)=g(k)$ is a decreasing function of $k$ (for the event that $a$ holds the record, an indicator of $\{k\le\text{constant}\}$).
Conditioning on $A$ multiplies the law of $n_b$ by $g$, which moves it downward in the likelihood-ratio order; conditioning further on $\{n_b>n/2\}$ restricts both laws to the same set and preserves the order.
Hence $n_b\mid A,\{n_b>n/2\}$ is stochastically at most $X\mid\{X>n/2\}$ with $X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\tfrac12)$; $p_i$ is a decreasing function of $n_b$, so $P(p_i\le u)\le P\{p(X)\le u\mid X>n/2\}\le u$. $\square$

The bound is exact at the levels the discrete $p$-value can actually take when the null holds with equality, the holder is fixed and $g=1$, and cautious otherwise.
We also check the proposition by brute force in the package's tests: on small boards we list every possible outcome, separately for each number of disputed tasks, for holder events that do and do not depend only on the total, including ``the holder was certified and a later challenger arrived'', and we include events that go the wrong way as negative controls, which must break the bound and do.
The proposition also covers the certified baseline, through a simple monotonicity property of the selective $p$-value.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Lemma 1.}
$p_{\mathrm{sel}}$ decreases when a task won by the challenger is added to the discordant set and increases when a task won by the holder is added.
\end{quote}
In words: more wins for the challenger make the step look stronger, more wins for the holder make it look weaker, which is what one would hope.
The proof is a short Pascal-rule computation (Appendix~\ref{app:lemma}).
Whether $a$ was certified against its own reference, and whether no later submission has been certified against $a$, both depend on $a$'s outcomes only through counts of this kind, so by Lemma~1 the event ``$a$ is the certified holder when $b$ arrives'' is increasing in $a$'s outcomes given the other systems, and Proposition~1 applies to it.

So the proposition covers both baselines: the leaderboard holder, which is simply the best so far, and the certified holder, which earned its place by passing an earlier test.
Its null is the conditional task-wise dominance null, which asks more than $\theta_b\le\theta_a$: when $b$ is a tweaked copy of an earlier system $c$, $b$ is likelier than $a$ to pass exactly where $c$ passed, the per-task probabilities sit on both sides of one half, and the proof says nothing, although every record may still be luck.
A rejection is therefore evidence against $a$ dominating $b$ task by task, the same reading as for McNemar's test; validity under the weaker null $\theta_b\le\theta_a$ alone is not proved, and Section~\ref{sec:valid} simulates the case where the two differ.
How strict the test is at the $i$-th record (its level $\alpha_i$) is set by one of three rules.
\emph{Alpha-spending:} $\alpha_i=6\alpha/(\pi^2 i^2)$, so that $\sum_i\alpha_i=\alpha$.
Think of $\alpha$ as a budget of allowed error, 5\% in all, handed out in shrinking pieces to the records as they arrive: the first record gets about 61\% of it, the second about 15\%, and so on, so the pieces never add up to more than the budget.
Its guarantee follows from Proposition~1 and the union bound (the chance that at least one of several things happens is at most the sum of their chances), with no independence assumed.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Corollary 1.}
Let the conditions of Proposition~1 hold for every pair that forms a record step on the leaderboard baseline, and let the $i$-th record step be tested at level $\alpha_i$ with $\sum_i\alpha_i\le\alpha$.
Then, for any submission calendar fixed in advance (same-date submissions handled by the per-date factor), any number of submissions and any dependence between steps, the probability that some record step at which the conditional task-wise dominance null holds is certified is at most $\alpha$.
\end{quote}
In words: hand out the error budget in pieces that add up to $\alpha$, and the chance that any false record is ever certified is at most $\alpha$, however long the chain gets and however the steps depend on one another.
\emph{Proof.}
Take first a submission order that does not depend on outcomes (distinct dates).
Let $E_i(a,b)$ be the event that the $i$-th record step has holder $a$ and challenger $b$.
It is the intersection of the record event $\{n_b>n_a\}$ with an event that does not involve $b$, depends on the outcomes of $a$ only through its total score and is increasing in it: $a$ scored higher than every submission before it, no submission between $a$ and $b$ scored higher than $a$, and the number of record steps before $a$, which is determined by the submissions before $a$, is $i-1$.
Proposition~1 applies with $A$ that event, so $P(p_i\le\alpha_i\mid E_i(a,b),\ldots)\le\alpha_i$ whenever the null holds for $(a,b)$; summing over the disjoint events $E_i(a,b)$ gives $P(\text{a null pair is certified at step } i)\le\alpha_i$, and the union bound over $i$ gives the result.
Under the best-first ordering the challenger is the best of the $g$ submissions on its date $\delta$, a selection the record event does not cover, and the set of submissions between $a$ and $b$ depends on $b$'s score; the per-date factor handles both.
Fix a holder $a$, a date $\delta$ with $g$ members and an index $i$, and let $A_i(a,\delta)$ be the event that $a$ holds the record among the submissions of dates before $\delta$, with $i-1$ record steps before it; it involves only earlier dates, so it is free of every member's outcomes, and it is increasing in $a$'s total.
A member $d$ can be certified at step $i$ only if $d$ beats $a$ and $p_d\le\alpha_i/g$, so Proposition~1, applied to the pair $(a,d)$ given $A_i(a,\delta)$ and $\{d\text{ beats }a\}$, gives $P(d\text{ is null and certified at step } i)\le(\alpha_i/g)\,P\{A_i(a,\delta)\cap\{d\text{ beats }a\}\}\le(\alpha_i/g)\,P\{E_i(a,\delta)\}$, where $E_i(a,\delta)=A_i(a,\delta)\cap\{\text{the best of }\delta\text{ beats }a\}$ is the event that step $i$ falls on date $\delta$ with holder $a$.
Summing over the $g$ members gives $\alpha_i P\{E_i(a,\delta)\}$; the events $E_i(a,\delta)$ are disjoint over $(a,\delta)$, so $P(\text{a null pair is certified at step } i)\le\alpha_i$, and the union bound over $i$ completes the proof. $\square$

Corollary~1 needs two things to be settled before the challenger's results are looked at: the level used at step $i$ and the event that put the holder in place.
Both hold for the leaderboard baseline, because how many records came before $a$ is decided by the submissions before $a$.
For the certified baseline, how many tests came before a challenger depends on how many submissions beat the certified holder, which depends on the holder's own results, so levels that count tests are not settled in this sense; levels that count calendar dates since the holder arrived are.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Corollary 3.}
Under the conditions of Proposition~1 for every tested pair, with the best-first ordering and the per-date factor, the certified-baseline procedure with the tenure-indexed levels $\alpha_{j,m}=\alpha\gamma_j\gamma_m$, where $j$ is one plus the number of certifications so far and $m$ the number of distinct submission dates since the certified holder's date, has a super-uniform $p$-value at every test and certifies some null pair with probability at most $\alpha$, for any dependence.
\end{quote}
In words: on the certified baseline, set the level from two counters that the challenger's results cannot touch, how many certifications have happened and how many dates have passed since the certified holder arrived; the whole-chain guarantee then holds.
\emph{Proof.}
With these levels the event that $a$ is the certified holder when $b$ arrives, that $a$ was certified against its own reference and that no later submission has been certified against $a$, involves $a$ only through counts of the kind in Lemma~1 at levels fixed by the calendar, so it is increasing in $a$'s outcomes and does not involve $b$; Proposition~1 gives per-step validity.
Given the conditioning, $j$ is determined by the submissions before the holder together with the holder event, and $m$ by the calendar, so the level is fixed; each pair $(j,m)$ is used by at most one date, the per-date correction handles the members of that date as in Corollary~1, and the levels used over any realised chain sum to at most $\alpha\sum_j\gamma_j\sum_m\gamma_m\le\alpha$. $\square$

The tenure rule spends a little budget on every date since the holder arrived, whether or not anything was tested that day, which costs power on long chains.
The spending and fallback rules that count tests instead have no such proof on the certified baseline, per step or over the chain, because the number of tests carries information about the holder's score; they are checked by simulation only.

\emph{Online fallback:} $\alpha_i=6\alpha/(\pi^2i^2)+\alpha_{i-1}\,\mathbf 1\{\text{record } i-1\text{ was certified}\}$.
In words: a record that passes passes its level on to the next record, so a run of genuine records builds up budget.
The rule is the fixed-sequence fallback procedure~\citep{wiens2003fallback} in the online form of \citet{tian2021onlinefwer}, who prove its family-wise error control for arbitrarily dependent $p$-values; it is never stricter than alpha-spending at any step, and Corollary~2 records that their guarantee carries over here once Proposition~1 supplies the per-step $p$-value and the per-date factor handles the ties.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Corollary 2.}
Under the conditions of Corollary~1, the online fallback rule certifies some record step at which the conditional task-wise dominance null holds with probability at most $\alpha$.
\end{quote}
In words: the first false record to be certified can only have inherited budget from genuine records just before it, and each genuine record's budget can be inherited only once, so the budget available to a first false certification adds up to at most $\alpha$.
\emph{Proof.}
Let step $i$ be the first null step certified.
Every step in the run of consecutive certified steps ending at $i-1$ is then non-null, so the level used at $i$ is at most $L_i=\alpha\sum_{k\in R_i}\gamma_k$, with $\gamma_k=6/(\pi^2k^2)$ and $R_i$ the maximal run of non-null steps immediately before $i$ together with $i$ itself.
$R_i$ is determined by the identities of the earlier pairs, which are fixed by the conditioning of Proposition~1 on $E_i(a,b)$ and the earlier submissions, so Proposition~1 gives $P(\text{step } i\text{ is the first null step certified})\le\E[\mathbf 1\{\text{step } i\text{ is null}\}\,L_i]$; with the per-date factor each member of the date is tested at $L_i/g$, and the per-date argument of Corollary~1 applies with $L_i$ in place of $\alpha_i$.
The runs $R_i$ of distinct null steps are disjoint, so $\sum_{i\text{ null}}L_i\le\alpha\sum_k\gamma_k=\alpha$ on every realisation of the chain, and summing over $i$ gives the result. $\square$
\emph{LORD++}~\citep{ramdas2017lord}: an online false-discovery-rate rule, which controls the expected share of certified records that are false rather than the chance of any false one, with level $\alpha_i=\gamma_i W_0+(\alpha-W_0)\gamma_{i-\tau_1}+\alpha\sum_{j\ge2}\gamma_{i-\tau_j}$, where $\tau_j$ is the index of the $j$-th certified record, $W_0=\alpha/2$, and $\gamma_j$ is any sequence with $\sum_j\gamma_j\le1$; we use $\gamma_j=6/(\pi^2 j^2)$, the same sequence as the spending rule, because the sequence published with LORD++ is built for streams of thousands of tests and leaves almost no budget for a chain of thirty records.
Each certified record earns budget back, so late records stay testable; LORD++ starts with half the spending rule's budget and overtakes it once records are certified.
Its guarantee is proved for independent $p$-values, and ours share tasks: alpha-spending and online fallback are the rules with the guarantee, and LORD++ is an alternative checked by simulation; \citet{javanmard2018online} give a more cautious variant for dependent $p$-values, which we have not implemented.
There are two choices for what to test against: each leaderboard record against the record it replaced, or every submission that beats the last \emph{certified} record against that record, so that the certified leader only changes when a record is certified, as in the Ladder~\citep{blum2015ladder}; Corollary~3 covers the second.
A normal-theory alternative that accounts for both selections at once (the polyhedral lemma applied to the event that $b$ is a record and $a$ was the holder) is described in Appendix~\ref{app:poly}; it needs a continuity correction on discrete scores and has no finite-sample guarantee, so it is reported only as a comparison.

\paragraph{Lag.}
For each record we ask how far back in the chain of records one must go before the new record is clearly better than that older record (same test, no correction).
A lag of one means the record is clearly better than the one it replaced; a lag of three means it is clearly better than the record three steps back but not the two in between.

\paragraph{Resolution.}
Resolution means the smallest true gap a benchmark can reliably see.
Let $s$ be the typical (median) standard deviation of the per-task differences between systems next to each other in the ranking, among the ten highest-scoring systems.
With $z=\Phi^{-1}(0.975)+\Phi^{-1}(0.80)$, the paired test at level 0.05 (two-sided; the selective record test has the same threshold up to the even-$n$ correction) detects a true gap of $\mathrm{MDD}=z\,s/\sqrt{T}$ (the minimum detectable difference) with 80\% power, meaning it will catch a gap that size four times out of five, and catching a gap $\Delta$ with that power takes $(zs/\Delta)^2$ tasks.
Redrawing the tasks with a bootstrap gives an interval for both numbers.
The smallest margin the test will call significant for a typical pair is about $1.96\,s/\sqrt{T}$, roughly 0.7 of the MDD.
This is a normal approximation~\citep{card2020power,kotawala2026resolution}; Section~\ref{sec:valid} checks it by simulation.

\paragraph{Rank intervals and top-rank frequency.}
We redraw the tasks with replacement many times, recompute every score and rank each time (tied systems share the better rank), and report the range that holds the middle 95\% of each system's ranks.
The top-rank frequency of a system is the share of redraws in which it has the top score, with ties split equally.
It is a frequency across redraws of the tasks, not the probability that the system is best.
Section~\ref{sec:valid} shows that the intervals contain the true rank when the true gaps are as big as the observed ones, but not when the leaders are truly tied, so the set below is the right statement to make about who could be best.

\paragraph{Set of possible best systems (confidence set of the best).}
Following multiple comparisons with the best~\citep{hsu1981mcb}, as adapted to paired bootstraps by \citet{jain2026lottery}, we keep every system that no rival beats by more than a margin set to cover all comparisons at once.
Let $\hat s_{ij}$ be the standard error of $\hat p_j-\hat p_i$ from $\hat\Sigma$ and $\hat p^*$ scores from one redraw of the tasks.
For each system $i$ let $M_i=\max_{j\ne i}\{(\hat p_j^*-\hat p_i^*)-(\hat p_j-\hat p_i)\}/\hat s_{ij}$, the furthest any rival moves ahead of $i$ in a redraw, in standard-error units.
System $i$ stays in the set if $\hat p_j-\hat p_i\le c\,\hat s_{ij}$ for all $j$, with $c$ the largest over $i$ of the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $M_i$.
A system that is truly best then stays in the set about $1-\alpha$ of the time, which is all the set promises; an earlier draft used the quantile of $\max_i M_i$, an all-pairs bound that gives larger sets, and we report both.
The guarantee is approximate and holds only for large samples, and in our simulations the set is on the cautious side.
The set says who cannot be ruled out, not who is likely to be best.

\paragraph{The winner's curse.}
Let $w=\arg\max_k\hat p_k$ be the system with the top score.
Its reported score $\hat p_w$ is too high on average, because $w$ was picked for having the highest score and part of a highest score is good luck.
This is the winner's curse.
We consider two corrections.

\emph{Conditional (polyhedral) method.}
Treat the scores as normal, $\hat p\sim N(\theta,\hat\Sigma)$, with $\hat\Sigma$ known.
The event that $w$ wins is $\{\hat p_j-\hat p_w\le 0,\ j\ne w\}$.
By the polyhedral lemma~\citep{lee2016exact,fithian2014optimal}, given this event and the part of $\hat p$ unrelated to $\hat p_w$, the winner's score follows a normal distribution with mean $\theta_w$ and variance $\Sigma_{ww}$ cut off to an interval $[V^-,V^+]$ whose ends depend only on the other systems' scores and on $\hat\Sigma$.
Inverting that cut-off normal distribution gives a median-unbiased estimate and a confidence interval for $\theta_w$ that remain valid after the winner was picked.
It is exact under normality, but it breaks down when the winner ties its runner-up, and nearly so when the margin is small, because the observed score then sits right at the edge of the cut-off and the interval runs off to infinity; two of our six real boards have an exact tie at the top.

\emph{Baselines.}
Two published corrections run alongside ours.
The hybrid of \citet{andrews2024winners} fixes the runaway-interval problem by first drawing a wide interval that covers every system's true score at once with confidence $1-\beta$, and then applying the conditional method inside that interval; the result can never run off to infinity, and the conditional level is adjusted to $(\alpha-\beta)/(1-\beta)$ so that overall coverage is still at least $1-\alpha$. Its estimate is median-unbiased under the hybrid distribution. We use $\beta=\alpha/10$.
The zoom correction of \citet{zrnic2025flexible}, in its simplest, locally simultaneous form~\citep{zrnic2024locally}, works in two stages: spend a share $\nu$ of the error budget on intervals for every system that all hold at once, keep the systems that could plausibly be the winner (those whose upper limit reaches the highest lower limit), then spend the remaining $\alpha-\nu$ on a joint interval over that smaller set alone; it corrects the interval, not the point estimate, and we use $\nu=\alpha/10$.
Both are exact when the scores are normal with known covariance; the joint quantiles are computed by Monte Carlo from $\hat\Sigma$.
\citet{hoff2025selective} is not implemented.

\emph{Iterated bootstrap.}
The bootstrap builds a stand-in world in which the observed scores are the truth, by redrawing the tasks with replacement.
In that bootstrap world we know every system's true score, so we can measure directly how much a winner's score is inflated by being the winner.
Draw a task resample, let $w^*$ be its winner, and set $\Delta^*=\hat p^*_{w^*}-\hat p_{w^*}$, how far the bootstrap winner beat its own bootstrap truth.
The plug-in correction is $\hat p_w-\hat b_1$ with $\hat b_1=\E^*\Delta^*$, the average of that gap~\citep{efron1993bootstrap,efron2014model}.
It does not remove all the bias, because the observed scores of the leaders are more spread out than their true scores, so being the winner is a smaller achievement in the bootstrap world than in reality.
A second round of bootstrap, run inside each resample, estimates the bias that remains: $\hat\theta_w=\hat p_w-2\hat b_1+\E^*\hat b_1^*$, where $\hat b_1^*$ is the plug-in bias computed from each outer resample.
The interval is $\hat\theta_w$ minus the quantiles of the centred $\Delta^*$.
It works for ties and when repositories rather than tasks are resampled.
Neither correction comes with a general theory here: the conditional method is exact only when the scores are normal with known covariance, and the iterated bootstrap is justified by how well it does in Section~\ref{sec:valid}, not by a theorem; taking a maximum is not a smooth operation, so the bootstrap is not guaranteed to work near ties.

\section{Validation}\label{sec:valid}
\subsection{Does the record test reject as often as it claims?}
A test at the 5\% level should wrongly call a lucky record ``clearly better'' no more than 5\% of the time.
We check this on simulated boards built to look like SWE-bench Verified: each task gets a difficulty from the real pass rates of the 30 strongest systems (standard deviation \NumSimSdLogit{} on the logit scale), system $k$ passes task $t$ with probability $\mathrm{sigmoid}(a_k+L_t)$, and the outcomes of different systems on the same task are linked through a Gaussian copula with correlation $\rho=\NumSimRho$, tuned so that the task-by-task spread between two equally able systems (\NumSimSdSim) matches the real board (\NumSimSdReal); real systems share base models and scaffolding, so they agree on far more tasks than shared difficulty alone would give.
Two null worlds, in which no system is really better than another, are simulated (Table~\ref{tab:recordtest}).
In the \emph{global null}, \NumSimK{} equally able systems arrive one after another, so every record is luck and the holder is always the running maximum (\NumNullR{} boards per size).
In the \emph{selected-step null}, holder and challenger are equally able and the holder is not inflated; we keep the pairs in which the challenger scored higher (\NumNullSelPairs{} pairs per size), which is the record condition.
At 500 tasks the one-sided test at 5\% wrongly rejects \NumNullSelOneB\% of selected steps, close to the $2\alpha$ predicted above, the two-sided test \NumNullSelTwoB\% and the selective test \NumNullSelSelB\%.
Under the global null the selective test's rate is \NumNullSelectiveB\% averaged over the chain, but the average hides where in the chain the step is: the first step, where the holder is simply the first submission and so only mildly lucky, rejects \NumNullPosFirstOneB\% one-sided and \NumNullPosFirstSelB\% selective, and later steps, against a holder that is the best of many, almost never (Supplement, Table~S1).
The selective test is at or below 5\% given the record event by construction, and the simulation agrees in every case, also with the model fitted to Lite, Terminal-Bench and LiveBench (Table~\ref{tab:recordtest}: with the model fitted to Lite the selective test wrongly rejects \NumRtestSelSelLite\% of selected null steps at 500 tasks, fitted to Terminal-Bench \NumRtestSelSelTB\% and fitted to LiveBench \NumRtestSelSelLB\%); the two-sided test differs from it only through the even-$n$ effect, and the one-sided test rejects too often.
The chance that Holm wrongly passes at least one step on a global-null board is \NumNullHolmB\%.
A second simulation checks the resolution formula: at the formula's minimum detectable difference, the selective test catches the gap \NumPowerAtMddSelB{} of the time at 500 tasks (Supplement, Table~S2), against a target of 0.80.

A split-half check on the real boards asks whether the test actually separates genuine records from lucky ones (Table~\ref{tab:split}).
For every record step we split the tasks into two random halves \NumSplitR{} times, run the test on one half, and record whether the challenger still beats the holder on the other half.
Steps that pass on the first half (\NumSplitSigShare\% of splits, against \NumSplitNullSigShare\% under the pure-luck reference below) beat the holder on the second half \NumSplitSigRep{} of the time and pass the test there \NumSplitSigRepSig{} of the time, against \NumSplitNonRep{} and \NumSplitNonRepSig{} for steps that did not pass on the first half.
Those raw figures need a reference point: every step is a record on the full data, so a challenger that did well on one half must have done less well on the other, and even a pure-luck record beats the holder on the second half more often than not.
We therefore simulate that pure-luck reference for each step with its own number of disputed tasks (fair-coin signs, conditioned on the record event on the full data, then the same split): it gives \NumSplitNullSigRep{} for first halves that pass and \NumSplitNullNonRep{} for those that do not.
Steps that pass repeat far above that reference, and steps that do not pass repeat at about it, on every board, which is what a test that separates genuine records from lucky ones should produce (on SWE-bench Test even the steps that do not pass sit above the reference, Table~\ref{tab:split}, the same message as the run split below: small real gaps the board cannot confirm); the pooled figures are dominated by SWE-bench Test, and Multimodal and Terminal-Bench contribute few passing splits.

Terminal-Bench allows a second kind of replication, across runs rather than across tasks: most submissions ran every task five times, so for each of its \NumRunSplitSteps{} record steps we split each task's runs at random into two parts, half the runs rounded down against the rest (two of five for most tasks), \NumRunSplitR{} times, run the test on the first part and check the second; a pure-run-noise reference redistributes each task's runs between the two systems at random, keeps the draws that form a record on the full data, and applies the same split.
Record steps that pass on the first part beat the old record on the second \NumRunSplitSigRep{} of the time (reference \NumRunSplitNullSigRep{}), and the steps that do not pass \NumRunSplitNonRep{} of the time (reference \NumRunSplitNullNonRep{}).
The second number is the informative one, with a caveat: it pools the \NumRunSplitNonSteps{} records that do not pass on the full data, and for \NumRunSplitAboveRef{} of them the replication rate sits clearly above the reference, while the two smallest margins sit at or below it.

Tuning on the public tasks is outside our model, but its simplest form can be priced (Table~\ref{tab:tries}).
Suppose a team tries $V$ equally able versions of its system on the benchmark and submits only the best one.
The record test allows for one challenger having scored higher, not for the best of $V$, so under the selected-step null it wrongly rejects \NumTriesSizeTwo\% of record steps at $V=2$, \NumTriesSizeFive\% at $V=5$ and \NumTriesSizeTen\% at $V=10$, and the share of submissions that set a record rises from \NumTriesRecOne\% to \NumTriesRecTen\%.
If the team declares $V$, multiplying the $p$-value by $V$ (the Bonferroni correction, $\min(1,Vp_{\mathrm{sel}})$) restores the level (\NumTriesCorrFive\% at $V=5$, \NumTriesCorrTen\% at $V=10$); an exact test for the best of $V$ would need the task-by-task results of all $V$ versions, which a leaderboard never sees, so when only $V$ is declared the Bonferroni correction is what is available.
On a chain in which every submission is the best of $V$, the holder is inflated in the same way and the chance of any false certification falls to \NumTriesFwerTen\% at $V=10$; the danger is one team trying many versions against a holder that did not.
This is why our reporting checklist asks for the number of versions tried: without it the true level of the record test is unknown.

\subsection{Do the certification rules keep their promise?}
We simulate boards of \NumChainK{} submissions on \NumChainT{} tasks with the fitted task difficulties and copula correlation, but with the true score started at \NumChainBase\% rather than the fitted \NumSimBase\%, to leave room for a staircase of improvements (\NumChainR{} boards per scenario; Table~\ref{tab:chainsim}); the lower base gives more disputed tasks per step, so the per-step rates here are not those of Table~\ref{tab:recordtest}, and validity holds in both.
Under the global null every record is luck.
In the staircase scenario the true score rises by \NumChainStep{} points every \NumChainPeriod{} submissions, so records are a mix of genuine jumps and lucky steps within a level; in the late scenario one genuine jump of twice that size happens halfway through the chain.
A certified step counts as false if the new system's true score is not higher than that of the system it was tested against.
Under the global null the unadjusted test certifies a false record on \NumChainNullUnadjFwer\% of boards, Holm over the finished chain on \NumChainNullHolmFwer\%, alpha-spending on \NumChainNullSpendFwer\%, online fallback on \NumChainNullFallFwer\%, LORD++ on \NumChainNullLordFwer\%, and the continuity-corrected polyhedral $p$-value under alpha-spending on \NumChainNullSpendPolyFwer\%; all at or below the 5\% level.
With one late jump of twice the step size, the share of genuine records certified is \NumChainLateHolmPower\% for Holm, \NumChainLateSpendPower\% for alpha-spending, \NumChainLateFallPower\% for online fallback, \NumChainLateLordPower\% for LORD++ and \NumChainLateSpendPolyPower\% for the polyhedral $p$-value under alpha-spending, with \NumChainLateHolmFdr\%, \NumChainLateSpendFdr\%, \NumChainLateFallFdr\%, \NumChainLateLordFdr\% and \NumChainLateSpendPolyFdr\% of the certified records being false; the unadjusted test certifies \NumChainLateUnadjPower\% of genuine records, but \NumChainLateUnadjFdr\% of what it certifies is false.
The staircase's jumps of \NumChainStep{} points sit right at the limit of what a \NumChainT{}-task board can see, and no rule with error control certifies more than \NumChainStairHolmPower\% of them (the unadjusted test certifies \NumChainStairUnadjPower\%); testing every submission that beats the last certified record, instead of only leaderboard records, raises the number of tests to \NumChainStairLordCertTests{} per board and the share certified to \NumChainStairLordCertPower\%, with \NumChainStairLordCertFdr\% of certifications false.
Online fallback matches alpha-spending in every scenario here, because genuine records never arrive in runs big enough to see, and by construction it never does worse; it pays off only where several records in a row are genuine and large, which none of the real boards below shows.
On the certified baseline, where every submission above the last certified record is tested, the tenure rule of Corollary~3 certifies a false record on \NumChainNullTenCertFwer\% of global-null boards and test-counting alpha-spending on \NumChainNullSpendCertFwer\%; on the staircase they certify \NumChainStairTenCertPower\% and \NumChainStairSpendCertPower\% of genuine steps, and with one late jump \NumChainLateTenCertPower\% and \NumChainLateSpendCertPower\%, the price of spending budget on dates with no test.
In the variant scenario every submission is equally able, but \NumChainVariantShare\% of the submissions after the first are tweaked copies of a random earlier submission (its results with passes flipped to failures at \NumChainVariantFlip\% and failures flipped to passes at the rate that keeps the true score the same), the case where the task-level null of Proposition~1 fails even though every record is still luck: the unadjusted test certifies a false record on \NumChainVariantUnadjFwer\% of boards, Holm on \NumChainVariantHolmFwer\%, alpha-spending on \NumChainVariantSpendFwer\% and LORD++ on \NumChainVariantLordFwer\%, so the rules stay at or below their level there too.
Alpha-spending has the stronger guarantee; LORD++ starts with half the budget and earns budget back on each certification, so it does worse when the only genuine record comes early and better on chains with several genuine jumps, as on the real boards below.

\subsection{Does the winner's-curse correction work?}
We test the corrections on SWE-bench Verified, with Lite and Terminal-Bench as further checks, in four settings.
\textbf{Known truth.} We treat the real table of results as the whole population, draw a pretend ``benchmark'' of $n$ tasks from it with replacement, and compare each estimate with the winner's true score on the whole population. This favours a bootstrap method, because the true gaps between systems equal the observed gaps.
\textbf{Equal leaders.} The hardest case: the top \NumBunchEqualTop{} systems are given exactly the same true ability, outcomes are simulated from the fitted task-difficulty model, and the truth is known.
\textbf{Held-out tasks.} The real 500 tasks are split at random into two halves; the winner is picked on the first half and the estimate is compared with its score on the second half, which is a fair but noisy stand-in for the truth.
\textbf{Held-out repositories.} The same, but the halves are made of different repositories, chosen among the \NumVThreeSplits{} repository subsets that put 40 to 60\% of the tasks in the selection half (\NumVThreeSelMin{} to \NumVThreeSelMax{} tasks). The selection board keeps its repository labels and the corrections resample repositories. The validation score is adjusted for the average difference in difficulty between the halves across all systems; the supplement (Table~S4) reports the unadjusted target and the task-resampling corrections as well.
Alongside our two corrections we run the hybrid and zoom baselines of Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

\paragraph{Results.}
In the known-truth runs the winner's reported score is too high by \NumVOneNCNaiveBias{} points at 500 tasks, \NumVOneNBNaiveBias{} at 250 and \NumVOneNANaiveBias{} at 100; with equally able leaders by \NumVBunchNCNaiveBias{} at 500 tasks and \NumVBunchNBNaiveBias{} at 250; and on real held-out tasks by \NumVTwoNaiveBias{} points.
After the iterated bootstrap correction \NumVOneNCIterBias{} points remain at 500 tasks (standard error \NumVOneNCIterSe), \NumVBunchNCIterBias{} with equal leaders and \NumVTwoIterBias{} on held-out tasks, so it removes about \NumVBunchNCReduction\% and \NumVTwoReduction\% of the inflation in the last two cases; the plain bootstrap removes \NumVTwoReductionPlain\% on held-out tasks.
These percentages carry simulation errors of about $\pm$\NumVTwoReductionSe{} points, and the held-out repetitions are different splits of one board, so they are not independent.
When the held-out half is made of other repositories, the repository-resampling iterated correction removes \NumVThreeReduction\%, so a benchmark whose tasks drift away from the ones the winner was picked on will still overstate its winner.
The correction removes the inflation but does not make the winner's score more precise: on held-out tasks the root mean squared error of the iterated bootstrap (\NumVTwoIterRmse) is not below that of the plain bootstrap (\NumVTwoBootRmse), because most of the error in one system's score is ordinary sampling noise, not selection.
On the common subset the conditional method, which is median-unbiased by construction, has a median error of \NumVBunchNCCondMedC{} with equal leaders against \NumVBunchNCIterMedC{} for the iterated bootstrap, but it is defined in only \NumVBunchNCCondFin\% of those runs.
The hybrid interval fails only at an exact tie for first and never runs off to infinity: with equal leaders it contains the truth in \NumVBunchNCHybCov{} of runs with a median length of \NumVBunchNCHybLen{} points, against \NumVBunchNCCondCov{} and \NumVBunchNCCondLen{} for the conditional interval, \NumVBunchNCIterCov{} and \NumVBunchNCIterLen{} for the iterated bootstrap, and \NumVBunchNCZoomCov{} and \NumVBunchNCZoomLen{} for the zoom interval, which leaves the point estimate as reported; the hybrid estimate's median error on the common subset is \NumVBunchNCHybMedC{} points.
The hybrid estimate came out identical to the conditional one in every setting we ran, because the wide first-stage interval seldom binds at these sample sizes; its gain over the conditional method is the shorter interval, and on held-out tasks it leaves \NumVTwoHybBias{} points of inflation against \NumVTwoIterBias{} for the iterated bootstrap.
The two baselines do not change the recommendation: the iterated bootstrap has the smallest bias among estimates that still work at a tie, and the hybrid interval is the one to report when a guarantee under normality is wanted.
In the known-truth runs the 95\% iterated interval contains the truth \NumVOneNAIterCov{} of the time at 100 tasks, against \NumVOneNANaiveCov{} for the interval around the reported score, and \NumVOneNCIterCov{} against \NumVOneNCNaiveCov{} at 500; it stays below the promised 95\% when tasks are few.
On Lite the iterated correction removes \NumLiteTwoReduction\% on held-out tasks but overshoots on held-out repositories (mean error \NumLiteThreeIterBias{} points against \NumLiteThreeNaiveBias{} for the reported score, with selection halves of only 120 to 180 tasks).
On Terminal-Bench (Supplement, Table~S5) the known-truth and equal-leaders results point the same way, but on real held-out halves of 44 tasks the iterated correction overshoots (mean error \NumTBTwoIterBias{} points against \NumTBTwoNaiveBias{} for the reported score), so the correction is not guaranteed to help on very small benchmarks.
On LiveBench (Supplement, Table~S5) equally able leaders are inflated by \NumLBBunchNaiveBias{} points, of which the iterated bootstrap leaves \NumLBBunchIterBias{} and the hybrid \NumLBBunchHybBias{}; on held-out halves of its questions there is no inflation to remove (\NumLBTwoNaiveBias{} for the reported score), because its leader is far ahead of the field, and the corrections leave it nearly untouched (\NumLBTwoIterBias{} and \NumLBTwoHybBias{}).

\subsection{Do the rank intervals and the set of possible best systems contain the truth?}
We use the known-truth setting again (\NumRankCovR{} repetitions; Table~\ref{tab:rankcov}).

When the top \NumBunchEqualTop{} systems are equally able, a leader's ``true rank'' is not well defined: if tied systems all count as rank 1 the interval contains it for only \NumRankBunchLeadA{} and \NumRankBunchLeadB{} of the leaders, if they count as the average of the tied ranks for \NumRankBunchLeadAvgA{} and \NumRankBunchLeadAvgB{}, and it overlaps the block of tied ranks in \NumRankBunchBlockA{} and \NumRankBunchBlockB{} of cases.

\paragraph{Run randomness on Terminal-Bench.}
Terminal-Bench runs each task several times, which lets us split the noise in a score into two parts: which tasks were drawn, and the agent giving different answers from one run to the next.
For the 20 strongest systems, run-to-run randomness is a median \NumTBRunShareSingle\% (range \NumTBRunShareSingleMin{} to \NumTBRunShareSingleMax\%) of the per-task variance of a single-run score, and \NumTBRunShareFive\% of a five-run score; for the difference between neighbouring top-10 systems it is \NumTBPairRunShareSingle\% with one run and \NumTBPairRunShareFive\% with five.
So run noise is a large part of the noise in a single-run coding-agent score, about a third of its variance and \NumTBPairRunShareSingle\% of the variance of a single-run difference between neighbouring systems, something the SWE-bench boards, with one run per submission, cannot show.

\paragraph{Development selection, seen through repeated runs.}
How much teams gain by privately trying several versions before submitting cannot be measured from one run per system, but the Terminal-Bench submissions that ran the whole benchmark several times show the scale of its simplest form.
\NumTBJobSubs{} kept submissions have at least two complete jobs (median \NumTBJobMedianJobs); the spread (standard deviation) of a submission's score across its jobs has median \NumTBJobSd{} points (middle half of submissions \NumTBJobSdLo{} to \NumTBJobSdHi), and a team that had reported its best job instead of the average of its jobs would have gained a median \NumTBJobInflMedian{} points (mean \NumTBJobInflMean, at most \NumTBJobInflMax).
As a rough guide to scale, consider a team that tries $V$ versions whose scores scatter like repeated runs and submits the best: the expected gain is $\E[\max_{v\le V} Z_v]$ times that spread, \NumTBJobInflVTwo{} points for two versions, \NumTBJobInflVFive{} for five and \NumTBJobInflVTen{} for ten, of the same order as the winner's-curse inflation from task sampling, and below this board's typical record margin (\NumTBJobInflVFive{} against \NumTBMedianMargin{} points).
The leaderboard reports the average over jobs, so this is a bound on what reporting the best run would add, not a finding that anyone did it, and picking among versions that differ by more than run noise can add more.

\paragraph{A non-agent comparison.}
The procedures are not special to agents, and LiveBench shows what they return on a leaderboard of language models scored automatically question by question (Table~\ref{tab:nonagent}).
With \NumLBT{} questions it can see gaps no finer than Lite can (minimum detectable difference \NumLBMDD{} points, about \NumLBTasksOnePt{} questions to see one point).
Its \NumLBSteps{} record steps under our main ordering win by a typical \NumLBMedianMargin{} points, above the board's critical margin of \NumLBCritMargin{}; \NumLBSig{} pass the unadjusted test, \NumLBHolm{} Holm, and the live rules certify \NumLBCertSpend{} (alpha-spending), \NumLBCertFall{} (online fallback) and \NumLBCertLord{} (LORD++), the last of those certifications within the Monte Carlo error of the sign-flip test ($p=\NumLBMarginP$ against a level of \NumLBMarginLevel), so that count is fragile; under the worst-first ordering of the \NumLBOnFirstDate{} models that share the first date there are \NumLBWorstSteps{} steps, \NumLBWorstSig{} of them clearly better.

\section{The honestboard package}\label{sec:tool}
\texttt{honestboard} (on PyPI as \texttt{pip install honestboard}; it needs only numpy and scipy) implements everything above.
A \texttt{Board} holds the systems-by-tasks table, with loaders for JSON, CSV and a checkout of the SWE-bench \texttt{experiments} repository.
\texttt{Board.check\_claim(system)} returns, in one call, the observed rank and its interval, how often the system ranks first, whether it could be the best, the paired test against its closest rival, and, for the leader, the winner's-curse-corrected score.
\texttt{Board.record\_audit()} walks through a leaderboard's history and tests every record step; \texttt{Board.resolution()} gives the smallest gap the board can see and the tasks needed for a one-point gap.
A command line (\texttt{python -m honestboard report BOARD.json}) prints the same, and \texttt{pr-check} reads a checkout of a leaderboard repository and prints a Markdown report on one new submission, so that a maintainer can post it on every pull request.
The package has unit tests that include simulations checking that the set of possible best systems and the conditional method contain the truth as often as promised, brute-force checks of the cut-off limits and of the sign-flip enumeration, tie handling, input validation and the covariance estimator.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Task sampling only.} We model luck in which tasks were chosen (Table~\ref{tab:scope}). Tuning on the public tasks is not modelled, and it is where much benchmark inflation comes from~\citep{singh2025illusion,yang2026hidden}; nothing here says how much of a leaderboard's progress is tuning; the best-of-runs bound from Terminal-Bench covers only the case where the versions tried differ by run noise, and the private-tries simulation prices the simplest form of tuning (Table~\ref{tab:tries}) but not training on the tasks. Run-to-run randomness is not modelled for SWE-bench, where submissions are single runs; on Terminal-Bench it is about a third of the variance of a single-run score (\NumTBRunShareSingleMin{} to \NumTBRunShareSingleMax\% across systems) and \NumTBPairRunShareSingle\% of the variance of a single-run difference between neighbouring systems.
\item \textbf{What the numbers are about.} Our statements are about more tasks of the benchmark's own kind, not about unseen repositories or real-world use. The held-out-repository results show how quickly the correction gets worse when the kind of task changes.
\item \textbf{Tasks are not independent.} Tasks from one repository move together. The task-level tests and intervals treat tasks as independent, which makes things look more certain than they are: with repository-specific strengths of the size the real board's standard errors suggest, the task-level record test wrongly rejects 10 to 25\% of lucky record steps (Table~\ref{tab:clustersim}). The repository-level test holds its level but has little power with a dozen repositories (Multilingual has 41), and its standard error comes out too small when one repository dominates; we report both counts and call the repository-level one the cautious one.
\item \textbf{Record test and certification.} Record steps are selected for being records. The selective test wrongly rejects at most 5\% under both nulls, at every chain position we simulated and on split halves of the real boards; it is exact for a fixed holder and cautious for a holder that is the best so far, under the task-level null (Proposition~1), which asks more than equal true scores and assumes outcomes independent across tasks; the case where that null fails (tweaked copies of earlier systems), the certified baseline with test-counting levels (no proof, per step or over the chain), and the polyhedral variant rest on simulation. The alpha-spending guarantee is exact given per-step validity but loses power late in a chain; LORD++'s guarantee assumes independent $p$-values, ours are linked through shared tasks, and we rely on simulation for it.
\item \textbf{No theory for the correction.} The iterated bootstrap is justified by simulation only; the conditional method is exact only under normality and breaks down at ties; the correction overshoots on the smallest benchmark; its intervals contain the truth less often than promised at small sizes. The hybrid and zoom baselines carry coverage guarantees under normality, and Section~\ref{sec:valid} reports how they compare.
\item \textbf{The comparison board.} LiveBench's judgments file covers three of its six categories, its questions change over time, and we date a model by its first judgment, so its record chain is only an approximation of the leaderboard's history; its per-question scores are partial credit rather than pass or fail.
\item \textbf{Rank intervals.} They show how much a rank moves when the tasks are redrawn and do not contain a true rank when leaders are tied; use the set of possible best systems for that question.
\item \textbf{Same-date ordering.} Which steps count as records depends on how same-date submissions are ordered; we report both extremes and prefer the one that invents no record. The best-first challenger is the best of its date, which the per-date factor of Section~\ref{sec:methods} corrects for; the proofs assume that, apart from this, which systems were submitted and in what order does not depend on their results, which a submission time finer than the day would make true without any correction.
\item \textbf{Missing task-by-task data.} \NumMissingN{} of \NumMissingFound{} Verified submissions have no task-by-task file. They are recent and score high; by overall score none would have held the record, but they are missing from every other analysis of that board, and other leaderboards may have similar gaps.
\item \textbf{Terminal-Bench handling.} Most job files do not say which benchmark version they ran, pooled submissions have uneven run counts, and runs that errored are counted as failures; other choices would move its numbers.
\item \textbf{No cross-benchmark test of the correction.} Few systems appear on two SWE-bench splits, they are old, and they are spread so widely that the winner is far ahead of the field, so there is no winner's curse to detect.
\end{itemize}

\section{Recommendations}
For benchmark maintainers: publish task-by-task results for every submission; call a record a new state of the art only when it is certified against the previous record by the selective record test, at a level set by the online fallback rule (or alpha-spending), whose guarantee needs no independence between steps, so that the error rate holds over the whole history, in the spirit of the Ladder mechanism~\citep{blum2015ladder}; LORD++ earns back more budget but is checked only by simulation here.
On a board of a few hundred tasks this means that most records of a point or two will never be certified; that is the honest state of affairs, and the argument for growing the benchmark.
Report the smallest gap the benchmark can see and the number of tasks needed to see a one-point gap, and expand or retire benchmarks that cannot see the gaps their leaderboards display.
For authors: report paired intervals against the strongest baseline, say how many versions were tried on the benchmark so that the record test can be corrected for it (Section~\ref{sec:valid}), and when claiming the top spot report the reported score, the winner's-curse-adjusted score and its interval together, with the hybrid interval where a coverage guarantee under normality is wanted, and say that tuning on public tasks is not covered.
For readers: treat a margin below the board's critical margin (about \NumVerifiedCritMargin{} points on Verified) as unresolved, not as a tie.
Appendix~\ref{app:checklist} lists ten things a leaderboard can adopt.

\section{Conclusion}
Leaderboards compress a large table of task-by-task results into one ranking.
On the four agent boards with at most 510 tasks and at least one record, most records cannot be told apart from the record before them by a test that allows for how records are selected; on the 2{,}294-task SWE-bench Test most can.
This is a statement about what the benchmarks can see, not a finding that the records are equal, and tuning on public tasks, which we do not model, is a separate source of inflation.
The procedures work unchanged on a language-model leaderboard, LiveBench, whose four records are too few to compare with the agent boards.
Task-by-task data, which several leaderboards already publish, is enough to say how much a record claim shows, and the live certification rules let a leaderboard say it at the moment the claim is made.

\section{Reporting checklist for leaderboards}\label{app:checklist}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Publish per-task outcomes for every submission, in one table with the same task identifiers.
\item Publish the full task list, including tasks that no submission solved.
\item State the number of runs behind each score and the trial structure.
\item State the number of variants, prompts or configurations tried against the benchmark before the submission.
\item Order same-date submissions by an explicit rule (for example submission time).
\item Report each record's margin over the previous record with a paired interval, not only the score.
\item Mark a submission as a new state of the art only if it beats the previous record in a selection-aware paired test at a stated level, and say which test.
\item Report the benchmark's resolution: the smallest difference it detects with 80\% power, and the number of tasks needed to resolve a one-point gap.
\item Report a confidence set of the best system next to the ranking.
\item Report the selection-corrected score of the current leader, and say that tuning against public tasks is not covered by the correction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proof of Lemma 1}\label{app:lemma}
Write $T_n(k)=\sum_{j\ge k}\binom nj$, so that $p_{\mathrm{sel}}(k,n)=T_n(k)/T_n(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1)$ for a challenger with $k>n/2$ wins among $n$ discordant tasks.
Pascal's rule gives $\sum_{j\ge k+1}\binom{n+1}{j}=2T_n(k)-\binom nk$ and $\sum_{j\ge k}\binom{n+1}{j}=2T_n(k)+\binom n{k-1}$.
The ratio $T_n(k)/\binom nk=1+r_k+r_kr_{k+1}+\cdots$ with $r_j=(n-j)/(j+1)$ decreasing in $j$ is decreasing in $k$, and so is $T_n(k)/\binom n{k-1}=r_{k-1}+r_{k-1}r_k+\cdots$.

\emph{A challenger win is added} ($k,n\to k+1,n+1$).
For even $n=2m$ the denominators are $T_n(m+1)$ and $2T_n(m+1)+\binom nm$, and the claim $\{2T_n(k)-\binom nk\}/\{2T_n(m+1)+\binom nm\}\le T_n(k)/T_n(m+1)$ reduces to $-\binom nk T_n(m+1)\le T_n(k)\binom nm$, which holds.
For odd $n=2m+1$ the denominators are $T_n(m+1)$ and $2T_n(m+1)-\binom n{m+1}$, and the claim reduces to $T_n(k)/\binom nk\le T_n(m+1)/\binom n{m+1}$ for $k\ge m+1$, which is the monotonicity of the first ratio.

\emph{A holder win is added} ($k,n\to k,n+1$).
For even $n=2m$ the claim $\{2T_n(k)+\binom n{k-1}\}/\{2T_n(m+1)+\binom nm\}\ge T_n(k)/T_n(m+1)$ reduces to $T_n(k)/\binom n{k-1}\le T_n(m+1)/\binom nm$ for $k\ge m+1$, the monotonicity of the second ratio.
For odd $n$ it reduces to $\binom n{k-1}T_n(m+1)+T_n(k)\binom n{m+1}\ge0$. $\square$

\section{A normal-theory selective p-value for a record step}\label{app:poly}
With $\hat p\sim N(\theta,\hat\Sigma)$, the event that $b$ is a record at position $k$ and $a$ was the holder is the polyhedron $\{\hat p_b>\hat p_a,\ \hat p_a\ge\hat p_j\ \forall j<k\}$, and the polyhedral lemma~\citep{lee2016exact} gives the truncated-normal law of the contrast $\hat p_b-\hat p_a$ on it, hence a one-sided selective $p$-value for $\theta_b\le\theta_a$.
Scores are pass counts over $T$, so they sit on a grid of spacing $1/T$, and the holder is often tied or one task apart from its closest earlier competitor.
Without a correction that puts the statistic right on the edge of the cut-off region and the $p$-value collapses: in our simulations it wrongly rejected 14\% of null steps at the 5\% level even with the true covariance.
Relaxing each constraint by half a grid step and taking the tail from half a step below the observed statistic (a continuity correction) brings the rate to 1 to 3\%.
We have no finite-sample guarantee for the corrected version and report it only as a comparison (Tables~\ref{tab:chainsim} and \ref{tab:chainaudit}).
